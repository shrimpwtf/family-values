# Simplicity: gradual revelation

## The problem

Complex products tend to show every feature all the time, whether or not you need it. That intimidates newcomers, and it does nothing for experts either.

## The principle

Keep the fundamentals at the user's fingertips and reveal everything else as it becomes relevant. Be accessible to newcomers **without sacrificing depth or power** for experienced users.

Simplicity is engineered, not found. A product with hundreds of possible paths, onboarding especially, only feels simple if each path is designed so that critical flows never turn into daunting tasks.

## The doorway model

Think of the interface as a series of connected rooms:

- From a few metres away you see part of a room through an open doorway.
- As you approach and enter, the space and its contents are gradually revealed.
- Each user action makes the interface unfold. **"I get to see where I'm going as I go there."**

Complexity stays out of sight and out of mind until it is needed.

## Rules

1. **Default view = fundamentals only.** List the 3-5 actions a user takes most often. Everything else is a candidate for deferral.
2. **Reveal on intent.** Secondary options appear in response to a user action (tap, notification, entering a context), not preemptively.
3. **Go deeper; don't jump away.** Prefer revealing detail *within* the current context (a tray, an expansion) over navigating to a new place. Users should feel they are diving deeper into where they are, not veering off course.
4. **Chunk overwhelming actions.** Break a complex task into compact, step-by-step pieces. A small surface signals that each step is approachable, and it encourages engagement without the commitment of a full screen.
5. **Focus per surface.** Each step carries one piece of content or one primary action.
6. **Respect cognitive load.** Treat an uncluttered, focused experience as something users are owed, not an aesthetic preference.

## Applying it

When simplifying a screen or flow:

1. Inventory every element and action on the screen.
2. Tag each one: *always needed*, *needed in context X*, or *rarely needed*.
3. Keep *always needed* visible. Move *context X* items behind the action that creates that context. Move *rarely needed* items into a tray or secondary surface reachable from where they are relevant.
4. Walk the edge-case paths (errors, empty states, first use, power-user shortcuts) and confirm that each one still reveals what it needs, when it needs it.
5. Check that nothing a user needs *right now* is more than one intentional action away.

## Anti-patterns

- Dashboards that expose every feature on day one.
- Onboarding as one long form instead of a guided sequence.
- Moving users to a separate screen for a quick confirmation, which strips away their context.
- "Simplifying" by deleting power features instead of deferring them.
